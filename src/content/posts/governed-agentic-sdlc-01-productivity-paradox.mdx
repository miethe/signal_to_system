## Each fix was correct. The same bug still took three sessions.

Between February and March 2026, the same FastAPI bug got fixed three times in <Term id="skillmeat">SkillMeat</Term>, one of my own projects, by three separate agent sessions working at three separate layers (API, web, tests).

Each fix was correct, but none of the sessions knew the others had already been there.

That's this whole essay at the size of a single bug: every session did its local job well, and the work still didn't add up.

I've been running AI coding agents across my own projects and teams for over a year now. Claude Code, Gemini, Codex, Copilot, and others. Let me be direct about what I'm actually seeing: the velocity gains at the individual task level are significant and repeatable. I can scaffold a new service in minutes that used to take days. Boilerplate evaporates. Test generation that used to be a Friday afternoon chore happens inline. Documentation that nobody wanted to write gets drafted before the PR is even open.

On specific, well-scoped tasks, I'm consistently seeing 5-10x+ speedups (my own read across that work, not a timed benchmark). Anyone who tells you AI coding tools don't deliver real productivity gains hasn't used them seriously (at least not in the last 6 months), or hasn't been honest about what the data shows at the task level.

And yet, when I look at the aggregate output across teams, the numbers tell a different story. Features shipped. Defects resolved. Time-to-production for meaningful work. Those metrics don't match the task-level gains. Not even close.

<PullQuote variant="thesis-marker">That's the paradox, and it's not a tooling problem.</PullQuote>

Task speed needs governed context, verification, and persistent operating state to compound. The three FastAPI sessions had the fixes; they didn't have the shared state.

So why can task speed fail to improve delivery?

I'm not alone in this. GitClear's 2024 *Coding on Copilot* report analyzed 150M+ lines of changed code and found that *code churn* (lines written then reverted or rewritten within two weeks) was projected to roughly double by 2024 against the pre-AI 2021 baseline, while net productive output didn't show a corresponding spike.[^gitclear] Google's internal studies suggest AI tools save measurable time on specific microtasks (code completion, test scaffolding, documentation) while leaving end-to-end delivery timelines largely flat. The most optimistic credible number I've seen for sustained team-level productivity improvement is around 20-30%, and that's in carefully structured environments. Most teams land well below that.

The METR 2025 randomized controlled trial on AI-assisted programming captures it precisely: experienced open-source developers using AI tools completed tasks 19% *slower*, despite self-reporting a perceived 20% speedup.[^metr] That's a 39-point perception gap between how fast they felt and how fast they actually were. They felt faster but delivered less. The tools compress time-to-first-draft while inflating time-to-verified-solution. That gap (subjective speed up, objective slowdown) isn't evidence that AI tools don't work. It's evidence of something more specific:

> Something structural is eating the gains between individual productivity and team-level outcomes.

That's the paradox, and it's not a tooling problem.

## The gains land where the spec is clear.

Scaffolding a new service in minutes and fixing one bug across three sessions are both AI-assisted work, but they don't land in the same place.

Before I diagnose the structural issue, a clarification on what AI coding tools actually deliver right now. The productivity tiers below describe **current state**: what you get without governance, without structured context injection, without the framework this series proposes. Think of them as a map of where the problems concentrate when teams adopt tools without adapting the system around them. The tiers and their multipliers are my classifications, not a benchmark.

| Impact tier | Where it lands | Typical tasks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High-confidence gains** (2–5x+ on the specific task) | Clear spec, small blast radius | Boilerplate and scaffolding; test generation for well-specified interfaces; documentation drafting; code translation between similar languages/frameworks; regex, SQL, and other "write once, verify once" artifacts |
| **Moderate gains** (meaningful but not transformative) | Context is recoverable, but not free | Bug investigation with good error context; refactoring within well-typed codebases; API integration with standard patterns |
| **Negligible-to-negative net impact** (without process changes) | Implicit context dominates; "plausible" isn't "correct" | Greenfield architecture decisions; cross-service integration work; anything requiring shared context the agent doesn't have; performance-sensitive code; security-critical paths |

AI tools compress *execution* time on tasks where the specification is clear and the blast radius is small. The higher you go in abstraction, from "write this function" to "design this system" to "coordinate this release," the less the tools help, and the more the surrounding governance matters.

These tiers aren't fixed. With proper governance (context injection, structured constraints, proactive guardrails), tasks that currently fall into the "negligible" bucket can move up. That's exactly what this series is about.

McKinsey's 2023 developer productivity research (the source of the "up to 50% faster" headline) breaks this down in the fine print: the largest gains were on code generation and documentation, while design, testing strategy, and cross-team coordination showed minimal improvement.[^mckinsey] DORA's 2025 *State of AI-Assisted Software Development* report is consistent: elite performers separate from the pack not on raw coding speed, but on deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate, and recovery time: system-level metrics, not individual ones.[^dora] AI tools, as currently deployed, mostly target the individual layer.

That points at an easy fix: tighten the spec, and the work moves up a tier. But the FastAPI bug was about as well-scoped as work gets (a 204 response with no body, and a `response.json()` call that throws on it), and each of the three sessions fixed it correctly. Clarity wasn't what they were missing; knowing it had already been fixed was.

## The harder problem: the gains don't compound.

A service scaffolded in minutes is the easy half.

The harder problem is why faster tasks don't compound into team-level outcomes when the task-level gains are real. The short answer: the system surrounding the tools wasn't designed to absorb the speed.

Call it productivity theater: visible, measurable output that *looks* like progress but doesn't correlate with delivery. Lines of code written. Pull requests opened. Tickets closed. These metrics were already suspect before AI entered the picture; now they're actively misleading. When an agent can generate 400 lines of plausible-looking code in 30 seconds, "lines written per day" stops being a proxy for anything.

The real constraint in most software organizations was never typing speed. It was (and remains) decision quality, coordination cost, review throughput, and integration risk. I don't think most teams have really internalized this yet. They see the task-level numbers and assume the system-level numbers will follow. AI tools don't automatically touch any of these. Or rather, they touch them in ways that can make things *worse* if the surrounding process doesn't adapt.

There are four failure modes I keep seeing. Each one looks like a tooling problem from the outside, but it's a governance problem underneath, which is why a better tool alone doesn't fix it.

### 1. The Volume Trap

Those 400 plausible-looking lines have to land somewhere. This is the most common failure, and the most insidious. The team adopts AI tools, individual output increases, more code enters the pipeline, but review capacity, test infrastructure, and deployment processes were sized for the old throughput. Larger PRs. Longer review queues. More integration conflicts. Eventually *slower* delivery, even though everyone feels busier. Adding AI to a team with existing process bottlenecks is like hooking a band up to an amplifier; it doesn't make the music better, it just makes the existing problems louder.

**What it looks like:** PR size creeps up 2-3x. Faros AI's Q4 2025 Engineering Impact Report, which analyzed 50,000+ pull requests, found review cycle times jumped roughly 91% in AI-heavy teams, nearly double the pre-AI baseline.[^faros] Merge conflicts spike. DORA's 2025 research statistically correlated AI adoption levels with delivery instability: a 7.2% increase in instability for every 25% bump in adoption, which compounds fast.[^dora] The team ships the same number of features per sprint but each one takes more rework.

**What to do instead:** Treat AI-generated code as higher-volume, not higher-trust. Tighten PR size limits, not loosen them. Invest in automated verification that scales with volume. Restructure review processes around risk tiers rather than uniform review depth. More on this in Post 3.

### 2. The Context Collapse

An agent can write code that passes every test and still break a rule nobody wrote down.

AI agents work with the context they're given, and most of the critical context in a mature codebase is implicit: architectural conventions, performance constraints, security boundaries, team norms about error handling. When an agent generates code that's syntactically correct and functionally plausible but violates an unwritten rule, the failure is silent until it's expensive.

**What it looks like:** AI-generated code passes tests but introduces subtle architectural drift. Service boundaries blur. Error handling becomes inconsistent. Tech debt accrues faster than before because it's *generated* faster than before. A 2025 arXiv study by Qiao et al. on brownfield AI-assisted development put numbers on this: developers were 48% faster at task execution, with no corresponding gain in codebase understanding.[^qiao] That gap (code accumulating faster than anyone can internalize it) is what I'd call <Term id="cognitive-debt">**Cognitive Debt**</Term>: the maintenance burden and loss of system comprehension that builds up when AI output outruns human understanding.

I've watched this accumulate in real time in my own work on SkillMeat. By late November 2025, I was juggling 3-4 concurrent personal projects, each with its own fragmented `.claude/` directory, and fixing a prompt rule in one project meant hunting for stale copies in the others. I wrote a Python script to batch-update the CLAUDE.md files across projects. It worked for about two weeks, then collapsed under its own complexity.

And by the time I noticed, the `.claude/` directory had grown to 1,703 markdown files across 50+ subdirectories. Every rule in `.claude/rules/memory.md`, `debugging.md`, `progress-cli-only.md` is a prohibition added because the mistake had already happened. That's Cognitive Debt made visible: not abstract drift, but a directory full of "don't do this again" notices.

Multiply that across five teams with five agents operating from different implicit assumptions, and you get a distributed coherence problem that no amount of after-the-fact code review can adequately catch.

**What to do instead:** Make implicit context explicit: ADRs agents can reference, coding standards that are machine-readable, context injection pipelines that feed constraints before generation. And accept that some work shouldn't be delegated yet, not because the tools can't do it, but because the context cost is too high.

### 3. The Accountability Gap

Take a PR where the AI wrote the code and the tests pass. Who owns it? In theory: the developer who prompted the agent. In practice: the accountability model dissolves fast. Developers rubber-stamp output they don't fully understand. Reviewers defer to "well, the AI wrote it and the tests pass." When something breaks in production, the post-mortem reveals that nobody actually *decided* the code should work that way; it was generated, accepted, merged, and deployed through a process that assumed human authorship at every step.

**What it looks like:** Post-mortems trace root causes to AI-generated code that nobody meaningfully reviewed. Rewire.it's 2026 *Case for Formal Verification in the Agentic Era* found at least 62% of AI-generated code contains security vulnerabilities (OWASP Top 10 exposure throughout, substantially worse than human baselines), and most of it sails through review unchallenged.[^rewire] Developers can't explain why their own PRs work. Knowledge of the codebase becomes shallow because understanding wasn't required to produce the code.

The FastAPI bug from the opening is this gap at its smallest. Each agent correctly identified the issue: FastAPI 204 No Content responses have no body, but calling `response.json()` on them throws a SyntaxError. Each agent fixed it in their layer. None of them knew the fix already existed somewhere else in the codebase. The three implementation commits are documented in a method note in Sources.[^fastapi-fix] Three agents, one bug, zero shared memory. That's the accountability gap: not malice or laziness, just a system with no mechanism for one session's fix to survive into the next.

The intervention isn't complicated: code ownership has to include comprehension, not just authorship. If a developer can't explain why their AI-generated PR works, it shouldn't merge.

### 4. The Shadow AI Problem

The failure mode nobody puts on a slide deck. Your developers are already using AI tools you don't know about: spinning up private Claude sessions, pasting proprietary code into ChatGPT, running local Copilot instances outside your managed environment. Roughly 40% of organizations already have Shadow AI in active use. IBM Consulting's 2025 research on AI governance found 86% have zero visibility into how those tools interact with their data flows.[^ibm-governance] Not hypothetical. Current state.

The security implications are concrete. Proprietary source code and API credentials leak to public model providers with unclear data retention policies. There's a relatively new attack vector called "slopsquatting," where bad actors register package names that AI models commonly hallucinate, turning AI-generated dependency lists into malware delivery mechanisms. IBM's 2025 *Cost of a Data Breach* report makes the cost explicit: 20% of data breaches are now classified as Shadow AI incidents, running roughly $670K more expensive per incident than traditional breaches.[^ibm-breach] We're looking at more than just a rounding error; it's what prohibition as a strategy actually costs you.

The uncomfortable part: banning AI makes this worse, not better. Developers reach for ungoverned tools because the official alternatives are slow, restrictive, or missing entirely. Locking down access won't reduce the risk; it just hides it. I can't point to a study for this, but the only intervention that actually shifts the behavior is offering governed alternatives that are *better* than the ungoverned ones. Not equivalent. Better.

## The infrastructure gap is the common root.

A bug fixed three times by sessions that didn't know about each other and a `.claude/` directory full of "don't do this again" notices look like separate problems, but they aren't. Rather, all four failure modes share a common root: existing SDLC governance was designed for a world where the bottleneck was code production. Every process artifact (sprint planning, code review, QA gates, change advisory boards) assumes code arrives at a roughly human pace, authored by people who understand it, in quantities that reviewers can meaningfully assess.

Those assumptions are wrong. And they're getting more wrong every quarter.

A useful lens for why this is happening now: software engineering has gone through two major infrastructure transitions in the last two decades. First, we moved from monolithic applications to distributed apps, and learned that distributed systems require new infrastructure primitives: service meshes, distributed tracing, eventual consistency patterns. The apps themselves didn't change what they did; the *environment* required a new engineering discipline.

Then we moved from distributed apps to distributed infrastructure, and learned the same lesson again. Kubernetes, Terraform, Platform Engineering, and the whole IDP movement emerged because you can't manage distributed infrastructure with the same tooling you used for a server room.

We're now in the middle of a third transition: from distributed infrastructure to distributed intelligence.

It took me embarrassingly long to see the pattern for what it was. Multiple agents, running in parallel, making decisions and generating artifacts across a codebase simultaneously, and I was racking my brain trying to govern it with tooling designed for the second transition. I wasn't alone in that (assuming a given company even made it that far). IBM and Arthur AI have started calling this emerging model the <Term id="adlc">**Agent Development Lifecycle (ADLC)**</Term>: the premise being that developing *with* agents requires different processes than developing *for* AI features.[^adlc] I agree with the diagnosis. What this series offers is the operator's version of the same shift: less framework branding, more of the specific patterns that have held up under real workloads. I place that work within the broader discipline of <Term id="agentic-systems-engineering">Agentic Systems Engineering</Term>, a name already in use in academic and enterprise work.[^ase]

We have Git for code, CI/CD for delivery, and Backstage for services. We have **zero** Platform Engineering for our cognitive infrastructure. No versioned context management. No agent observability. No trust tiers for AI-generated artifacts. No memory that persists across sessions. We have built elaborate infrastructure for everything except the thing we're now asking to generate the infrastructure.

Consider what a typical gate-based governance model actually does: it creates synchronization points where human judgment is applied to human-produced artifacts. Code review gates assume the reviewer can hold the full context of a PR in their head. QA gates assume test coverage reflects intentional design decisions. Release gates assume that if the prior gates passed, the aggregate risk is managed.

Each of these assumptions degrades when AI tools enter the picture:

- **Review gates** break when PR volume exceeds review capacity, leading to either rubber-stamping or bottlenecking (both of which defeat the purpose).

- **QA gates** break when AI generates tests alongside code, because the tests may validate the implementation's behavior rather than the *intended* behavior. You get high coverage numbers masking specification gaps.

- **Release gates** break when the velocity of merged code outpaces the ability to assess cumulative risk. Each individual change looked fine; the aggregate introduced a subtle regression nobody caught.

Gate-based governance is *reactive*: it inspects artifacts after they're produced. That worked when production was slow enough for inspection to keep up. It doesn't work when an agent can produce in an afternoon what used to take a sprint.

What's needed isn't more or faster gates. It's governance that's *proactive*, embedded into the generation process itself, constraining what gets generated rather than scrambling to inspect what was generated.

## Governed agentic treats agents as participants, not accelerators.

Go back to those three FastAPI sessions. Each agent did real work, and none of them knew the fix already existed somewhere else in the codebase. I'll lay out the full framework across this series, but the core idea deserves to be stated plainly up front:

Governed agentic development treats AI agents as first-class participants in the SDLC, with explicit roles, constraints, and accountability boundaries, rather than treating them as invisible accelerators behind a human interface.

Let me be direct about what this series is *not*: it's not about <Term id="vibe-coding">**Vibe Coding**</Term>: non-engineers prompting LLMs without regard for what the code actually does or how it holds up in production. That's a different activity for a different audience. What I'm describing is <Term id="agentic-engineering">**Agentic Engineering**</Term>: professional engineers operating AI agents within a deliberate, governed framework. If you're accountable for software that runs in production, this series is for you.

Something has to decide what context an agent receives, what constraints it operates under, how its output gets verified, and how what it learned persists past the session. That layer is the central concept here, and it's what I call the <Term id="harness-engineering-control-plane">**Harness Engineering Control Plane**</Term> for agentic development. Just as Kubernetes introduced a control plane to manage distributed workloads, separating the "what should run" from the "how it runs," a governed agentic SDLC needs a layer that manages cognitive workloads.

The <Term id="agent-amnesia">**Agent Amnesia**</Term> problem illustrates exactly why this matters. When an AI agent finally figures out a complex legacy business rule (the specific edge case in your payment service, the subtle constraint in your data model), that knowledge dies the second the session closes. The next developer starts from zero. This isn't a model problem; models don't have persistent memory by design. It's an infrastructure problem: we have no mechanism for capturing, versioning, and re-injecting what agents learn.

That leaves the question the next essay in this path picks up: what has to survive a session, and in what shape, for the next one to act on it?

And that's just the memory side. The observability gap is worse, because you don't find out you're bleeding until someone happens to complain. In SkillMeat, a component called DiffViewer was silently re-parsing every diff on every render. For three months! Tests passed. The feature worked. I only caught it when users started complaining about UI lag on large syncs. The fix itself was a few lines (documented in a method note in Sources[^diffviewer-fix]), but nothing in the process would have surfaced the problem on its own. "Tests passed" was the only signal I had, and tests don't measure render cost or token burn. That's not a model failure. It's what happens when agent-generated code ships into production with no observability layer underneath it.

I've since addressed this observability gap with another project, <Term id="ccdash">**CCDash**</Term>, which I'll post about in the future. Its scope and coverage aren't established here.[^ccdash]

In a governed agentic world, the reframe is structural: **human intent becomes the new source code, and agent sessions become the runtime.** The question stops being "how do we review AI-generated code faster" and starts being "how do we express intent precisely enough that the generated code is correct by construction." Context (the architectural decisions, coding conventions, security boundaries, and domain knowledge that shape correct generation) becomes the environment the runtime executes in. And like any runtime environment, it needs to be versioned, tested, and deployed.

In practice, this means:

- **Agents operate under defined scopes**, not unlimited generation. An agent tasked with writing a data access layer gets the schema, the ADRs for that service, and the team's error-handling conventions, not free rein to generate whatever looks right.

- **Verification is proportional to trust**, not uniform. AI-generated boilerplate gets lightweight automated checks. AI-generated business logic gets human review with comprehension requirements. AI-generated security-sensitive code gets the same scrutiny as human-authored code in that path (which is to say, a lot). And these tiers shift upward as governance matures.

- **Governance is embedded, not appended.** Rather than reviewing AI output after the fact, constraints are injected into the generation context. The agent knows the rules before it writes, rather than a reviewer catching violations afterward.

- **Context is a first-class artifact.** The CLAUDE.md files, architectural specs, ADRs, and coding standards that shape agent behavior are versioned, governed, and deployed like any other infrastructure component, not scattered across local directories and wiki pages.

I've been iterating on versions of this for over a year now, in my own projects and on teams where I'm accountable for the outcomes. Parts 2-5 break down the specific patterns, tooling, and organizational shape that make it hold up at scale.

Those three FastAPI sessions didn't need a faster model; each one found the bug and fixed it correctly. [^ase]: Sun et al., [Robust, Observable, and Evolvable Agentic Systems Engineering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.20729), and [OutSystems' Agentic Systems Engineering announcement](https://www.outsystems.com/de-de/news/outsystems-agentic-systems-engineering-announcement). These establish existing usage of the term; this essay makes no claim to it.

[^gitclear]: GitClear (2024). [*Coding on Copilot: Data Shows AI's Downward Pressure on Code Quality*](https://www.gitclear.com/coding_on_copilot_data_shows_ais_downward_pressure_on_code_quality). Analysis of 153 million lines of code changed between 2023 and 2024.

[^metr]: METR (2025). [*Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity*](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/). Randomized controlled trial documenting the divergence between perceived and actual developer speed. Preprint: [arXiv:2507.09089](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089).

[^mckinsey]: McKinsey Digital (2023). [*Unleashing developer productivity with generative AI*](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/mckinsey-digital/our-insights/unleashing-developer-productivity-with-generative-ai). Productivity breakdowns by task type: code generation, documentation, refactoring, and design.

[^dora]: DORA (2025). [*State of AI-Assisted Software Development Report*](https://dora.dev/research/2025/dora-report/). Google Cloud / DORA research. Found that for every 25% increase in AI adoption, delivery instability rose by an estimated 7.2% and throughput dropped an estimated 1.5%.

[^faros]: Faros AI (2025). [*The AI Productivity Paradox in Software Engineering*](https://www.faros.ai/ai-productivity-paradox). Analysis of pull request review cycle times and delivery metrics in AI-influenced codebases.

[^qiao]: Qiao, Y., Hundhausen, C., Haque, M. U., & Shihab, E. (2025). [*Comprehension-Performance Gap in GenAI-Assisted Brownfield Programming: A Replication and Extension*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02922). arXiv:2511.02922.

[^rewire]: Richardson, T. (2026, February 4). [*When AI Writes the Code, Verification Becomes the Job*](https://rewire.it/blog/when-ai-writes-the-code-verification-becomes-the-job/). Rewire.it Blog. Finds that while 80%+ of developers now use AI assistants for code generation, at least 62% of AI-generated code contains vulnerabilities, shifting the engineer's primary job from writing code to formally verifying it.

[^ibm-governance]: IBM (2025). [*Ethics and governance of agentic AI*](https://www.ibm.com/think/insights/ethics-governance-agentic-ai). Plus IBM's [agentic governance platform launch](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2025-06-18-ibm-introduces-industry-first-software-to-unify-agentic-governance-and-security).

[^ibm-breach]: IBM Security (2025). [*Cost of a Data Breach Report 2025*](https://www.ibm.com/reports/data-breach). Findings on the Shadow AI breach premium and the 20% share of breaches classified as Shadow AI incidents.

[^adlc]: Arthur AI (2025). [*Introducing the Agent Development Lifecycle (ADLC)*](https://www.arthur.ai/blog/introducing-adlc). Plus IBM whitepaper [*AI Agents Demand a New Development Lifecycle*](https://www.ibm.com/forms/mkt-whitepaper-f42e5).
