## What happened

An ARC strategy council ran against the AOS dark-factory business thesis
(`arc-run-2026-08-07-aos-dark-factory-thesis-strategy`, outcome `narrow_scope`, 16 findings).
Its strongest accepted finding, RED-02, was that **green-over-untested is a systemic property of this
system**: health signals report `ok` over paths that have never carried load, and for a service sold
on "human attention only at gates that matter," that is an inability to honour the core promise —
because the operator's assurance and the customer's assurance come from the same false green.

The owner then signed off on the run's adjudication. I recorded the sign-off and, treating it as
closure, set `arc_certification.yaml` to `certificationState: accepted`.

`arc validate` refused it, with three cross-artifact contract errors:

```
accepted certification lockHash is not bound to a resolved council lock
accepted certification has unresolved disputed adjudication
accepted certification has an unresolved high-risk finding
```

All three were correct. The run has no `resolved_council.lock.yaml` (it was not `--project` bound),
one finding is deliberately disputed pending an external scan, and nine high-severity findings have
their validations still outstanding. `accepted` would have been a green certification over
validation paths that had never been exercised.

**That is RED-02. I committed the run's own strongest finding, inside the run's own closeout, roughly
thirty minutes after writing it up.**

## Lesson 1 — a human signature and a machine gate are different assertions

Owner sign-off settles **judgment**: which findings stand, at what severity, what the recommendation
is. `certificationState: accepted` asserts **state**: all gates passed, nothing unresolved. The
second is strictly stronger, and here it was not true and *was not supposed to be yet* — the whole
point of the validation plan is that the work is outstanding.

The trap is structural, not careless. Both the human decision and the machine condition terminate in
a field whose value is the word "accepted," so a signature reads like it satisfies the condition. Any
schema where an approval enum and a gate-outcome enum share a vocabulary will invite exactly this
substitution.

**Generalizable rule: when a human approval and a machine gate write to the same field, the human's
signature will be used to satisfy the machine's condition — and the machine's condition is usually
the stronger claim.** Either separate the fields, or make the gate refuse, which ARC does.
Sign-off belongs in the decision record; certification state belongs to the gates.

## Lesson 2 — naming a failure mode confers no protection against it

This is the part worth carrying forward. I had just written four paragraphs about false assurance,
built a four-instance evidence table for it, and recommended a standing rule that *a gate must report
`unproven` rather than `ok` until it has carried load once*. None of that stopped me from producing a
green certification over unexercised paths at the first opportunity.

Articulating a defect and being immune to it are unrelated. The mechanism caught it; the
understanding did not. That is the argument for mechanizing doctrine rather than documenting it,
restated from the inside — and it is the same conclusion `mode-d-enforcement.md`,
`artifact-provisioning.md`, and `shared-checkout-safety.md` each reached after prose alone failed.
**The failure mode you just finished describing is not thereby behind you. Assume you are one
convenient default away from committing it.**

Corollary for review closeouts: the closeout is a high-risk moment specifically *because* it feels
like the work is done. Flipping a state field to its terminal value is the cheapest possible action
and the one most likely to overclaim.

## Lesson 3 — evidence decayed five times during a single review, including on the review's own output

The council's own accepted finding ADJ-01 was that the thesis's evidence pack had gone stale within
24 hours. During the review and its follow-up edits, the shared-checkout guard reported **four
separate HEAD drifts** in the launchpad — 11 commits from other agents — and each one required
re-deriving claims before acting:

| Drift | What moved | Effect on a claim I was about to act on |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 commits incl. `op day` | CPS-02 re-verified unchanged; author distribution aged 457/1 → 461/1 (autonomy ratio 0.218% → 0.216%) |
| 2 | `fix(autopilot)` | CPS-02 re-verified again at `c43dc31`; no effect |
| 3 | 6 commits incl. `aos-attest` M1+M2 | `node_01KZD7VH93H5A45FBRTJQH5BP3` moved `not_started` → `in_progress` — the node ADJ-01 is tracked under. The remedy started while the finding was being written. |
| 4 | `fix(gates)` | no effect |

Two of the four were caught by the guard denying a mutating op; two by voluntarily running
`aos-git status` first. Drift 3 is the sharpest: a finding asserting "this is tracked and unstarted"
became wrong while the ink was wet, so the published text now names the landing commit (`5fb46ba`)
rather than a status — **a commit sha is a durable fact; a status is a reading.**

Practical consequence adopted in the artifact: dated measurements must be *labelled* as dated. The
`457 / 1` figures stay in the run because they were true when probed, with `DC-14` recording the
re-derivation and the convention.

## Lesson 4 — an over-broad guard was load-bearing by accident

The git guard fired on the **launchpad** when my mutating command was `ssh node 'cd other-repo && git
commit'` — a different repository on a different host. Strictly, that is a scope false positive: the
guard matched `git` in the command string and evaluated drift against the local checkout.

It was still the right outcome, because two of my findings cited launchpad source files
(`orchestrate.py:93`, `arc.py:231`) and the launchpad had genuinely moved. Had the guard scoped
itself precisely to the repo being mutated, it would have stayed silent and I would have committed a
run whose E3 evidence I had not re-checked.

Worth recording without over-learning from it: **do not "fix" this guard's matching to be
repo-precise without first asking what it would stop catching.** The class it caught here — mutating
repo A while reasoning from repo B, which moved — is real, common in this multi-repo system, and not
covered by anything else. `shared-checkout-safety.md` warns that false positives train a reflexive
`pin --repin`; this is the opposite case, where breadth beyond the mutation target earned its keep.

## What changed as a result

- `certificationState` stays `pending` on the run; the sign-off lives in `decision_record.md`, with a
  dedicated section explaining why the two are different assertions.
- `decision_record.md` records the refused `accepted` attempt and its three gate errors, in the
  record itself, rather than quietly reverting.
- `evidence_pack.md` gained `DC-14`, recording the re-derivation and establishing the dated-measurement
  convention for every figure in the run.
- The thesis-spec revision names commits rather than statuses wherever a status would decay.
- `trace_bundle.jsonl` carries the rejected-transition event, so the attempt is auditable and not just
  narrated.

## Open

Nothing here needs a code change. The gates that mattered already existed and worked. The candidate
generalization for the doctrine layer is Lesson 1 — **approval-vocabulary collision between human and
machine fields** — which is a schema-review question worth asking of the other subsystems that carry
both an approval and a gate outcome (IntentTree `HumanRequest` resolution, the HAI resume gate,
SkillMeat promotion). None has been checked.
