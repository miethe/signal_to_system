## Lessons

### L1 — An open question blocking work may already be answered in a sibling repo. Check before deciding.

The plan listed OQ-3 as hard-blocking M2. It was already **decided and shipped**: PF-1's own
`decisions[]` had accepted "stable asset id, replace-in-place, no supersedes-chain", and the shipped
delivery-dossier convention matched. The correct action was to *inherit and cite*, not to deliberate.

**Why it matters:** in a multi-repo initiative, an OQ recorded in repo C can be closed by work in
repo A without anyone editing C's plan. Plan-file open questions go stale silently and in the
expensive direction — they make you re-decide something, and a fresh decision can *fork* you from a
sibling that already shipped the other answer.

**How to apply:** before treating a cross-repo OQ as open, grep the sibling repos' `decisions[]` and
milestone titles for it. PF-1's milestone was literally named "Dossier revisioning convention".

### L2 — When two open questions look contradictory, the contradiction is usually one field doing two jobs.

Pre-flight surfaced what looked like an incoherence: OQ-3 wanted `revision` to mean "replace in
place"; OQ-5 wanted `revision` to be the per-instance discriminator. Both cannot hold.

The resolution was not to pick a side but to **name the missing concept**. Adding an explicit
`instance_key` let both rules compose cleanly — same instance re-published → refresh in place;
different instance → distinct row. The apparent conflict was an artifact of overloading `revision`.

**How to apply:** two requirements that contradict over a single field are evidence of a missing
field, not of a genuine trade-off. Look for the unnamed concept before negotiating.

### L3 — Verify a field is *maintained*, not merely *present*, before keying identity off it.

The tempting OQ-5 answer was `external_id = report:{route}:{subject}:{revision}` — `revision` was
right there in the envelope. But `report.revision` is set to `1` by `init_manifest` and **nothing in
any of the three repos ever increments it**; the bump is prose-only instruction in a mode doc. Keying
durable pointer identity off an LLM-bumped field would have reproduced the exact DI-283 collapse it
was meant to fix, one level deeper and much harder to see.

**How to apply:** before a field becomes an identity component, grep for its *writers*, not its
readers. "The field exists" and "the field is maintained" are different claims.

### L4 — Two review rounds in the same defect class means the guard is on the wrong layer.

Round 1: `link_identity` fell back to the collapsing bare form, reachable via a non-atlas export
target. Round 2: `route_of()` did no normalization, so `"PROGRAM"` / `"program "` defeated every
exact-match guard — including the fix from round 1.

Both were the same class: *an identity guard defeated by validation scoped too narrowly.* The
same-class stop rule fired correctly — no third review. The fix was a **design change**: canonicalize
`route` once at ingress and reject unknown routes loudly, replacing three independent
re-interpretations of a free string.

**How to apply:** the second occurrence is the signal, not the second bug. Two rounds of "add a check
where the leak appeared" means a missing canonicalization boundary. Also: my original contract wording
caused round 1 by scoping enforcement to the *export target* rather than the *identity layer* — a
contract can encode the same layering mistake as code, and then it propagates to every implementer
who follows it.

### L5 — A "valid fixtures only" test helper cannot find a missing-validation bug.

30 passing tests missed the round-1 defect because `make_envelope()` always produced a well-formed
envelope. The gap was only visible by reading code against the contract.

**How to apply:** when the thing under test is a *guard*, the fixture helper must be able to emit
malformed inputs. Assert on "no side effect occurred" (zero subprocess calls), not just "an exception
type was raised".

### L6 — Infer nothing about an enum you can read.

The first M2 pass inferred `PHASE_TYPES = {atomic_task}` from a tier narrative. `milestone` is a real
`NodeType`, and the live phase nodes of **this initiative's own PF-1 leg** are `milestone`-typed — so
the guardrail would have rejected every legitimate `phase` link. It failed *safe* but it failed
*always*, which is the worst kind of guard: it looks correct and makes the feature inert.

**How to apply:** pin allow-sets to the enum's source file with a line citation. A fail-safe guard
that always fails is indistinguishable from a working one until someone notices nothing happens.

### L7 — Check whether the *installed* binary has the verb, not just whether the code is on main.

PF-2's `itt link report` is merged to `intenttree` main, but the installed `itt` is a frozen
`uv tool` snapshot without it. "Shipped upstream" and "callable here" are different facts. Treating
verb-absence as an offline case (a probe, not a crash) turned a latent breakage into a clean skip —
and fleet CLIs drift constantly, so this is the general posture, not a workaround.

### L8 — An agent must not self-approve a governance gate, even when instructed to complete the work.

U5 touches `.claude/rules/`, gated behind `METIS_IDENTITY_CHANGE_APPROVED=1`. The implementing agent
declined to self-approve; the orchestrator did too. The change was preserved as
`u5-governance-gated.patch` (matching two existing precedents) and the AC recorded as **8/9, not
9/9**.

**How to apply:** a delegated prompt saying "complete all nine surfaces" cannot manufacture an
approval the gate exists to require. Preserve the work, report the shortfall precisely, and let the
completion claim be smaller than the instruction.

### L9 — Reordering milestones to put docs last removes a whole class of drift.

The plan put M4 (docs) in wave 1 as "fully independent". It wasn't: M4's U2 documents the hook M3
creates, including its file name and env contract. Sequencing M4 last cost one wave and removed the
choice between inventing names in advance or re-editing prose afterwards.

### L10 — Refreshing the CLI turned two "shipped" features into two live defects. Faked boundaries cannot certify an integration.

Post-merge, once the `itt` CLI was refreshed (`link report` finally present), the very first live run
found **two defects that 93 passing tests had certified as working**:

1. **`--json` is a GLOBAL `itt` option and must precede the subcommand.** `publish_report.py` passed
   it after (`itt node get ID --json`), which exits 2 with `No such option '--json'`. So every node
   read returned `None`, the guardrail concluded every node was missing, and it **rejected every
   link**. The write path had the same bug. Fails safe, fails *always* — indistinguishable from a
   working guardrail from the outside.
2. **The `program` route was unusable for the node shape we actually use.** It resolved only to an
   ancestor of type `root|pillar|work_area`, else raised — but a per-initiative node is a root-level
   `work_package` with `parent_id: null`, which is what PF-1, PF-2 *and* PF-3 all are. It failed
   identically for a correct anchor and a deliberately wrong one.

Both were invisible to the suite because **the fakes were more permissive than the real tool** — they
accepted `--json` at any position and returned JSON regardless. This is the same failure family as a
prior recorded lesson (an offline fake echoing `system` where the server returns `source_system`), and
it recurred anyway.

**How to apply:** a fake must be at least as strict as the real tool, and ideally *assert* the real
tool's constraints — the fake now rejects `--json` anywhere but `argv[1]`, so this cannot regress
silently. And an integration is not verified until it has run against the real dependency once. "93
tests pass" certified the code against my model of the CLI, not against the CLI.

### L11 — The two defects above were mine to cause, structurally: I deferred the one cheap check.

The stale-`itt` problem was *known and recorded* during pre-flight (D5) and deliberately parked as an
ops action. Parking it was defensible — but it meant the entire link half of the feature shipped
having never once executed against its actual dependency, and both live defects sat in exactly that
untested gap. The AC-2 "guardrail rejects a wrong scope" evidence was faked-boundary only, and the
`program` route's total failure could not have survived a single real invocation.

**How to apply:** when a dependency is unavailable, the honest record ("no live round trip") is
necessary but not sufficient — weigh whether making it available *is itself the cheapest next step*.
Refreshing the CLI took one command and immediately paid for itself twice. A deferral that blocks all
live verification of a milestone's core mechanism deserves more suspicion than a deferral that
merely postpones polish.
